Internal Energy

Every substance is made of particles (atoms, ions or molecules) that are always moving and always interacting with their neighbours. Internal energy is the name given to the total energy stored inside a substance because of this.

Definition

Internal energy is the sum of the random kinetic energies and the potential energies of all the particles in a substance.

  • Kinetic energy — from the random translational, vibrational and rotational motion of particles.
  • Potential energy — from the intermolecular/interatomic forces (bonds) holding particles near or to each other.

Two things change internal energy: heating a substance (which speeds up particle motion — raises kinetic energy) or changing its state (which stretches or breaks bonds between particles — changes potential energy, at constant temperature).

Common misconception

Temperature is not the same as internal energy. Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of particles. Internal energy is a total — it depends on temperature and on how much substance you have. A bathtub of lukewarm water has far more internal energy than a spark at 1000°C, because it contains vastly more particles.

Practice questions

1. Define internal energy, identifying its two components.
Cr: A, iL 1–2
Model answer Internal energy is the sum of the random kinetic energy (due to particle motion) and the potential energy (due to the forces/bonds between particles) of all the particles in a substance.
2. Two identical mugs contain water — one at 40°C, one at 90°C, with the 90°C mug only a third full. Analyse which mug has the greater internal energy, and explain your reasoning.
Cr: A, iiL 5–6
Model answer It cannot be decided from temperature alone. The 90°C water has a higher average particle kinetic energy, but the 40°C mug contains three times the mass (three times the number of particles). Internal energy depends on both temperature and mass/particle number, so the larger, cooler mass could plausibly hold more total internal energy — a full numerical comparison would need the specific heat capacity and mass of each sample.

Kinetic Theory of Gases

The kinetic theory is a model that explains the behaviour of gases by assuming they are made of tiny particles in constant, random motion.

The postulates (assumptions) of kinetic theory

  1. Gases consist of a very large number of particles in continuous, random motion.
  2. The particles themselves occupy negligible volume compared with the volume of the container.
  3. Collisions between particles (and with the container walls) are perfectly elastic — no kinetic energy is lost overall.
  4. There are no intermolecular forces between particles, except during the instant of collision.
  5. The average kinetic energy of the particles is directly proportional to the absolute (Kelvin) temperature.

Evidence for kinetic theory: Brownian motion

We cannot see individual gas or liquid molecules, so how do we know they are really moving randomly? The key evidence is Brownian motion.

The pollen-grain / smoke-cell experiment

When a large, visible particle — a pollen grain suspended in water, or a smoke particle suspended in air — is viewed under a microscope, it is seen to move in a continuous, random, zigzag path, even though nothing appears to be pushing it.

  • The pollen grain itself is far too large to be moved by a single molecule.
  • It is bombarded on all sides, at every instant, by huge numbers of much smaller, invisible molecules (water or air) moving randomly.
  • At any given instant, more molecules happen to strike from one side than another. This unequal, unbalanced bombardment gives the grain a net push in a random direction.
  • Because the imbalance is constantly changing, the direction of the net push keeps changing too — producing the random zigzag path we observe.

Scientific judgment: the zigzag motion of the visible particle is indirect but conclusive evidence that the invisible fluid molecules around it are themselves in continuous, random motion — exactly as kinetic theory assumes. We are seeing the effect of molecular motion, not the molecules themselves.

Temperature and Brownian motion

Raising the temperature of the fluid increases the average kinetic energy of its molecules (postulate 5 above). Faster-moving, more energetic molecules strike the visible particle harder and more often, so the zigzag path becomes more vigorous — larger, faster, more erratic steps.

Practice questions

1. A student observes a suspension of pollen grains in water through a microscope and notices the grains moving in random, zigzag paths. Analyse this observation to explain the evidence it provides for the kinetic theory of matter. Make a scientifically supported judgment about what causes this specific movement.
Cr: A, ii, iiiL 7–8
Model answer The pollen grain is visible under the microscope, but the water molecules surrounding it are not — they are far too small to resolve optically. The grain moves because it is constantly and randomly bombarded by these invisible, fast-moving water molecules from every direction. Since the number and force of impacts from each side is never perfectly balanced at any instant, the grain experiences a net resultant force that keeps changing in size and direction, producing the observed random zigzag path. This is strong evidence for kinetic theory because it shows indirectly that the fluid is made of discrete particles in continuous, random motion — exactly what the kinetic model predicts, even though the molecules themselves cannot be seen. (This phenomenon is called Brownian motion.)
2. A student wants to investigate how changing the absolute temperature T of air inside a smoke cell affects the Brownian motion of smoke particles.
(a) Describe a clear, testable scientific question for this investigation.
(b) Design a method, clearly stating the independent, dependent and two controlled variables, how data will be collected reliably, and one safety measure.
Cr: B, i, iii, ivL 5–6
Model answer (a) Research question: "How does increasing the absolute temperature of the air inside a smoke cell affect the average speed of the random (Brownian) motion of the smoke particles, as observed under a microscope?"

(b) Method outline:
  • Independent variable: absolute temperature of the air in the smoke cell (heated using the electric heating element in steps, e.g. 293 K, 303 K, 313 K, 323 K, 333 K), measured with the digital thermometer.
  • Dependent variable: the average speed / degree of erratic movement of a smoke particle, estimated by tracking one particle's position on a grid at fixed time intervals (e.g. every 2 seconds for 30 seconds) and calculating average displacement per interval.
  • Controlled variables: (i) the concentration/amount of smoke introduced into the cell, and (ii) the light intensity of the bright light source used to illuminate the particles.
  • Reliability: repeat the tracking of the same particle (or several particles) three times at each temperature and calculate a mean displacement, discarding anomalous readings.
  • Safety: allow the electric heating element and smoke cell to cool before handling, and keep the bright light source away from the eyes / do not view it directly.
3. State two assumptions of the kinetic theory of gases, and outline why real gases deviate from these assumptions at high pressure.
Cr: A, i, iiL 3–4
Model answer Two assumptions: (1) particles occupy negligible volume compared to the container, and (2) there are no intermolecular forces except during collisions. At high pressure, the gas is compressed into a much smaller volume, so the actual volume of the particles is no longer negligible compared to the container, and the particles are forced much closer together, so intermolecular forces become significant. Both assumptions break down, and the gas deviates from ideal behaviour.
03

The Gas Laws

The gas laws describe how pressure (P), volume (V) and absolute temperature (T, in kelvin) of a fixed mass of gas relate to each other. Each law holds one variable constant.

LawHeld constantRelationshipKinetic theory explanation
Boyle's Law Temperature \(P_1V_1 = P_2V_2\) Smaller volume → particles hit the walls more often per second → higher pressure.
Pressure Law (Gay-Lussac's) Volume \(\dfrac{P_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2}{T_2}\) Higher T → particles move faster → hit the walls harder and more often → higher pressure.
Charles's Law Pressure \(\dfrac{V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{T_2}\) Higher T → particles move faster; volume must expand so wall-collisions stay frequent/hard enough to keep pressure constant.
Combined Gas Law — \(\dfrac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2V_2}{T_2}\) Used when P, V and T can all change together.

Always use absolute temperature

All gas law calculations require temperature in kelvin, never °C — because the relationships are direct proportions to absolute temperature. Convert with \(T(\text{K}) = T(°\text{C}) + 273\).

Worked example

A gas occupies 2.0 m³ at a pressure of 100 kPa and 27°C. It is compressed to 0.5 m³ and heated to 127°C. Find the new pressure.

T₁ = 27 + 273 = 300 K    T₂ = 127 + 273 = 400 K

\[ \frac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2V_2}{T_2} \;\Rightarrow\; P_2 = \frac{P_1V_1T_2}{T_1V_2} = \frac{100 \times 2.0 \times 400}{300 \times 0.5} \]

P₂ = 533.3 kPa

Practice questions

1. State Boyle's Law in words and as an equation.
Cr: A, iL 1–2
Model answer At constant temperature, the pressure of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its volume: \(P_1V_1 = P_2V_2\).
2. A sealed syringe holds 40 cm³ of air at 101 kPa and 20°C. It is pushed down to 25 cm³ while its temperature is kept constant. Calculate the new pressure.
Cr: A, iiiL 5–6
Model answer \(P_1V_1 = P_2V_2 \Rightarrow P_2 = \dfrac{101 \times 40}{25} = 161.6\text{ kPa}\)
3. Explain, using kinetic theory, why the pressure of a gas in a sealed, rigid container increases when the gas is heated.
Cr: A, iiL 3–4
Model answer Heating increases the average kinetic energy of the gas particles, so their average speed increases. Since the container is rigid, volume cannot change, so the faster particles collide with the walls both more frequently and with greater force per collision. Since pressure results from the total force of these collisions per unit area, pressure increases.
04

Conditions for the Ideal Gas Approximation

An "ideal gas" is a theoretical gas that perfectly obeys the kinetic theory assumptions and the gas laws at all pressures and temperatures. Real gases only approximate this behaviour under certain conditions.

ConditionWhy it makes the approximation better
Low pressureParticles are far apart, so their own volume is negligible compared with the container, and intermolecular forces are weak.
High temperatureParticles move fast enough that weak intermolecular attractions have negligible effect on their motion.

When gases deviate most from ideal behaviour

Real gases deviate significantly from the ideal gas model at high pressure and low temperature — conditions found, for instance, just before a gas condenses into a liquid. Here, particle volume is no longer negligible and intermolecular attractive forces become significant, both of which are ignored by kinetic theory.

Practice questions

1. Identify two conditions under which a real gas behaves most like an ideal gas.
Cr: A, iL 1–2
Model answer Low pressure and high temperature.
2. Discuss why a gas is least likely to behave ideally just before it condenses into a liquid.
Cr: A, iiL 5–6
Model answer Just before condensation, the gas is typically at low temperature and often high pressure, so particles are close together and moving slowly. This means intermolecular attractive forces (which kinetic theory assumes are negligible) become strong enough to noticeably affect particle motion, and the particles' own volume is no longer negligible compared with the space between them. Both core assumptions of the ideal gas model fail, so behaviour deviates most from ideal at this point.
05

Conduction — Qualitative Analysis

Conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through a material (or between materials in contact) without any overall, bulk movement of the material itself. It happens through particle-to-particle interactions.

Mechanism

  • In all materials: particles at the hot end vibrate more vigorously and collide with neighbouring particles, passing on kinetic energy step by step through the material.
  • In metals specifically: conduction is much faster because metals also have free (delocalised) electrons that gain kinetic energy at the hot end and move rapidly through the metal, transferring energy far more quickly than vibration alone.

The rate of thermal energy transfer by conduction depends on three factors:

FactorEffect on rate of conduction
Type of materialMetals (free electrons) conduct much faster than non-metals/insulators (vibration only, e.g. wood, plastic, air).
Cross-sectional areaA larger cross-sectional area gives more particles/electrons a path to transfer energy simultaneously, so the rate increases.
Temperature gradientA steeper temperature difference over a given distance increases the rate of energy transfer — energy flows faster from hot to cold when the difference is larger.

Practice questions

1. Explain why metals are much better conductors of thermal energy than non-metals.
Cr: A, iiL 3–4
Model answer In non-metals, thermal energy is transferred only through particle vibrations passed between neighbouring particles, which is relatively slow. Metals additionally contain free (delocalised) electrons, which can move rapidly through the whole structure carrying kinetic energy away from the hot region much faster than vibration alone, making metals much better conductors.
2. Two identical rods of the same metal are heated at one end. Rod A has double the cross-sectional area of Rod B. Predict and justify which rod conducts thermal energy faster.
Cr: A, iiL 5–6
Model answer Rod A conducts faster. A larger cross-sectional area provides more particles and free electrons across the width of the rod to transfer thermal energy at the same time, increasing the overall rate of conduction, even though both rods are the same material and length.
06

Specific Heat Capacity

Different substances need different amounts of energy to heat up by the same amount. Specific heat capacity (c) quantifies this.

Definition & formula

Specific heat capacity is the energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1°C (equivalently, 1 K).

\[ Q = mc\Delta T \]

Q = thermal energy transferred (J)  ·  m = mass (kg)  ·  c = specific heat capacity (J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹)  ·  ΔT = temperature change (°C or K)

SubstanceSpecific heat capacity (J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹), approx.
Water4 200
Ice2 100
Aluminium900
Iron450
Copper390

Worked example

How much energy is needed to raise the temperature of 2.5 kg of water from 20°C to 80°C? (cwater = 4200 J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹)

\[ Q = mc\Delta T = 2.5 \times 4200 \times (80-20) = 2.5 \times 4200 \times 60 \]

Q = 630 000 J = 630 kJ

Practice questions

1. Define specific heat capacity, stating its SI unit.
Cr: A, iL 1–2
Model answer Specific heat capacity is the energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1°C. Its SI unit is J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ (or equivalently J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹).
2. A 0.4 kg aluminium block absorbs 18 000 J of energy and its temperature rises by 50°C. Calculate the specific heat capacity of aluminium from this data, and compare your answer with the accepted value.
Cr: A, iii · Cr: C, iiL 5–6
Model answer \(c = \dfrac{Q}{m\Delta T} = \dfrac{18000}{0.4 \times 50} = 900\text{ J kg}^{-1}\text{°C}^{-1}\). This matches the accepted value for aluminium (≈900 J kg⁻¹°C⁻¹) closely, suggesting the experimental data is reliable.
3. Equal masses of water and iron start at the same temperature and are heated with the same amount of energy. Analyse which one will reach a higher final temperature, and why.
Cr: A, iiL 5–6
Model answer Iron will reach a higher final temperature. Since \(\Delta T = Q/(mc)\), and iron has a much lower specific heat capacity (≈450) than water (≈4200), the same energy input produces a much larger temperature rise in iron than in water, for equal masses.
07

Latent Heat

When a substance changes state (melts, freezes, boils, condenses), its temperature stays constant even though energy is still being transferred. This energy is called latent heat — it changes the potential energy of the particles (breaking or forming bonds), not their kinetic energy.

Definition & formula

Latent heat is the energy required to change the state of a substance without any change in temperature.

\[ Q = mL \]

Q = thermal energy (J)  ·  m = mass (kg)  ·  L = specific latent heat (J kg⁻¹)

  • Specific latent heat of fusion — energy per kg to change between solid and liquid (melting/freezing).
  • Specific latent heat of vaporization — energy per kg to change between liquid and gas (boiling/condensing). This is always larger than latent heat of fusion, since far more bonds must be fully broken (not just loosened) to separate particles into a gas.

Reading a heating curve

On a temperature–time graph of a substance being heated at a constant rate, the temperature rises steadily (specific heat capacity applies), then flattens completely during a change of state (latent heat applies — all the energy goes into breaking bonds, none into raising temperature), then rises again once the change of state is complete.

Worked example

Calculate the total energy needed to convert 0.2 kg of ice at 0°C completely into water at 0°C. (Specific latent heat of fusion of ice = 334 000 J kg⁻¹)

\[ Q = mL = 0.2 \times 334000 \]

Q = 66 800 J = 66.8 kJ

Practice questions

1. Explain why the temperature of boiling water stays at 100°C even while it continues to absorb energy from a heat source.
Cr: A, iiL 3–4
Model answer During boiling, the energy absorbed is used entirely as latent heat — to overcome the intermolecular forces holding the liquid particles together so they can separate into a gas. This energy increases the particles' potential energy, not their kinetic energy, so the average particle speed (and therefore the temperature) does not change until the change of state is complete.
2. How much energy is needed to boil away 0.05 kg of water already at 100°C? (Specific latent heat of vaporization of water = 2 260 000 J kg⁻¹) Calculate your answer.
Cr: A, iiiL 5–6
Model answer \(Q = mL = 0.05 \times 2260000 = 113000\text{ J} = 113\text{ kJ}\)
3. Sketch and describe (in words) the shape of a temperature–time graph for a solid heated at a constant rate until it becomes a gas, and label which sections involve specific heat capacity and which involve latent heat.
Cr: A, iL 3–4
Model answer The graph rises steadily (solid warming — specific heat capacity), then flattens at the melting point (solid → liquid — latent heat of fusion), then rises steadily again (liquid warming — specific heat capacity), then flattens at the boiling point (liquid → gas — latent heat of vaporization), then rises again (gas warming — specific heat capacity). The two flat sections are the state changes; the three rising sections are where specific heat capacity applies.
08

Applications of the High SHC of Water

Water's specific heat capacity (≈4200 J kg⁻¹°C⁻¹) is unusually high compared with most common substances. This means water heats up slowly and cools down slowly for a given energy transfer — a property put to use in many everyday and engineering contexts.

ApplicationHow the high SHC of water is exploited
Car radiators / industrial cooling systemsWater circulates through an engine, absorbing large amounts of thermal energy with only a modest temperature rise, then releases it elsewhere — an efficient coolant.
Coastal vs. inland (continental) climatesThe sea absorbs and releases large amounts of energy for a small temperature change, so coastal regions have milder, more stable temperatures year-round than inland regions at the same latitude.
Sea breezes / land breezesLand heats up (and cools down) faster than the sea during the day/night because it has a much lower SHC, driving convective air movement between land and sea.
Hot water bottlesWater stores a large amount of thermal energy per kg for a given temperature, and releases it slowly over time, making it effective for sustained warmth.
The human bodyThe body is largely water, which helps buffer core temperature against rapid fluctuations from external or internal changes.

Practice questions

1. Explain, in terms of specific heat capacity, why coastal cities tend to have smaller temperature differences between day and night than cities far inland.
Cr: A, iiL 5–6
Model answer The sea has a much higher specific heat capacity than land, so for the same energy gained from the sun during the day (or lost at night), the sea's temperature changes far less than land's. Air near the coast is influenced by the sea's stable temperature, moderating the local climate and reducing the day–night temperature range compared with inland areas dominated by land, which heats and cools quickly.
2. Suggest why water, rather than a metal like copper, is used as the coolant in most car engines, despite copper being a much better thermal conductor.
Cr: C, iiL 5–6
Model answer Water's role is to absorb and carry away large quantities of thermal energy while flowing as a liquid, which requires a high specific heat capacity so its own temperature does not rise too quickly (and cause it to boil) while doing so. Copper's high thermal conductivity is useful for spreading heat through a solid quickly, but it is not a fluid that can circulate and physically transport energy away from the engine the way water can.

Thermal Radiation

Unlike conduction and convection, thermal radiation is the transfer of energy by electromagnetic waves, and needs no medium — it can travel through a vacuum (this is how energy reaches Earth from the Sun). Every object above absolute zero emits thermal radiation.

The black body

A black body is an idealised object that absorbs all electromagnetic radiation falling on it (reflecting none), and is therefore also the most efficient possible emitter of radiation at any given temperature.

Stefan–Boltzmann Law

\[ L = \sigma A T^4 \]

L = luminosity / power radiated (W)  ·  σ = Stefan–Boltzmann constant (5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴)  ·  A = surface area (m²)  ·  T = absolute temperature (K)

Because power depends on \(T^4\), even a small increase in temperature causes a very large increase in radiated power.

Luminosity and apparent brightness

Luminosity (L) is the total power radiated by an object (e.g. a star) in all directions — an intrinsic property of the object itself, independent of the observer's distance.

Apparent brightness (b) is how bright the object appears from a given distance — it decreases with the square of the distance, since the same total power spreads out over an ever-larger sphere:

\[ b = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2} \]

b = apparent brightness (W m⁻²)  ·  L = luminosity (W)  ·  d = distance to the source (m)

Emission spectrum & Wien's Displacement Law

A hot object emits a continuous spectrum of electromagnetic wavelengths (a black-body spectrum), but the intensity peaks at one particular wavelength. Hotter objects peak at shorter wavelengths (bluer light); cooler objects peak at longer wavelengths (redder light).

\[ \lambda_{max}T = \text{constant} \approx 2.9\times10^{-3}\ \text{m K} \]

λmax = wavelength of peak emission (m)  ·  T = absolute temperature (K)

Worked example

A star has a surface temperature of 6000 K and a radius of 7 × 10⁸ m. Calculate its luminosity, and the wavelength at which it emits most strongly.

A = 4πr² = 4π(7×10⁸)² ≈ 6.16×10¹⁸ m²

\[ L = \sigma A T^4 = (5.67\times10^{-8})(6.16\times10^{18})(6000)^4 \approx 4.5\times10^{26}\text{ W} \] \[ \lambda_{max} = \frac{2.9\times10^{-3}}{6000} \approx 4.83\times10^{-7}\text{ m} \approx 483\text{ nm (blue-green light)} \]

Practice questions

1. Distinguish between conduction and thermal radiation as methods of energy transfer.
Cr: A, iL 1–2
Model answer Conduction transfers thermal energy through particle collisions and requires a material medium (it cannot happen through a vacuum). Thermal radiation transfers energy via electromagnetic waves and requires no medium at all — it can travel through a vacuum, such as space between the Sun and Earth.
2. Two stars have the same luminosity, but Star X is twice as far from Earth as Star Y. Compare their apparent brightnesses as seen from Earth.
Cr: A, iiL 5–6
Model answer Since \(b = L/(4\pi d^2)\) and luminosity is equal, apparent brightness is inversely proportional to \(d^2\). Star X is twice as far away, so \(d_X = 2d_Y\), giving \(b_X = L/(4\pi(2d_Y)^2) = \tfrac{1}{4}\cdot L/(4\pi d_Y^2) = \tfrac{1}{4}b_Y\). Star X appears one quarter as bright as Star Y.
3. Star A appears bluish-white and Star B appears reddish. Analyse, using Wien's Law, which star has the higher surface temperature and explain your reasoning.
Cr: A, iiiL 7–8
Model answer Star A has the higher surface temperature. Wien's Law (\(\lambda_{max}T = \text{constant}\)) shows that peak emission wavelength is inversely proportional to temperature. Blue-white light has a shorter wavelength than red light, so the star with the shorter peak wavelength (Star A, bluish-white) must have the higher absolute temperature, while Star B's longer, redder peak wavelength indicates a lower surface temperature.
4. A metal sphere's absolute temperature is doubled while its surface area stays the same. Calculate the factor by which its radiated power increases, according to the Stefan–Boltzmann law.
Cr: A, iiiL 5–6
Model answer Since \(L = \sigma A T^4\) and only T changes: doubling T multiplies power by \(2^4 = 16\). Radiated power increases by a factor of 16.
