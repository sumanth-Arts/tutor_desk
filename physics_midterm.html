SI Units & Dimensional Analysis

The SI system (Système International) is the standard set of units used in science. Every physical quantity is either a fundamental (base) quantity, with its own defined unit, or a derived quantity, whose unit is built by combining base units.

Base quantitySI unitSymbol
Lengthmetrem
Masskilogramkg
Timeseconds
Electric currentampereA
TemperaturekelvinK
Amount of substancemolemol
Luminous intensitycandelacd

Derived units

A derived unit is built from base units through a defining equation. E.g. speed = distance/time → unit = m/s. Force = mass × acceleration → unit = kg·m/s², which is given the special name newton (N).

Dimensional analysis

Dimensional analysis expresses a quantity in terms of the dimensions of mass [M], length [L], and time [T]. It is used to (a) check whether an equation is physically consistent, (b) derive the unit of an unfamiliar quantity, and (c) convert between unit systems.

Worked example: Check that \(v^2=u^2+2as\) is dimensionally consistent.

\[ [LT^{-1}]^2 \;\overset{?}{=}\; [LT^{-1}]^2 + [LT^{-2}][L] \] \[ [L^2T^{-2}] = [L^2T^{-2}] + [L^2T^{-2}] \;\checkmark \]

Every term has the same dimensions, so the equation is dimensionally consistent (though this method cannot confirm the numerical constants, like the "2", are correct).

Practice questions

1. Identify the SI base units of mass, length and time, and derive the SI unit of force from F = ma.
Practice
Model answer Mass: kg, length: m, time: s. Since F = ma, the unit of force = kg × (m/s²) = kg·m·s⁻² = N.
2. Use dimensional analysis to check whether \(s = ut + at^2\) (without the ½) could still be dimensionally valid, and explain what this tells you about the limits of dimensional analysis.
Practice
Model answer [L] = [LT⁻¹][T] + [LT⁻²][T²] = [L] + [L], which is still dimensionally consistent even without the ½. This shows dimensional analysis can confirm an equation's structure is plausible, but it cannot detect missing or incorrect pure numbers (like ½), since numbers have no dimensions.

Recap: Distance, Displacement, Speed, Velocity & Acceleration

QuantityTypeDefinition
DistanceScalarTotal length of the path travelled.
DisplacementVectorStraight-line distance from start to end, in a stated direction.
SpeedScalarDistance travelled per unit time.
VelocityVectorDisplacement per unit time, in a stated direction.
AccelerationVectorRate of change of velocity: \(a=(v-u)/t\).

Quick check

A runner completing one lap of a track has travelled a large distance, but their displacement is zero, since they return to the start.

Practice question

A cyclist rides 400 m east, then 150 m west. Calculate (a) the total distance travelled, and (b) the final displacement.
Practice
Model answer (a) 400+150 = 550 m. (b) 400−150 = 250 m east.
03

Displacement–Time Graphs

Gradient of a d–t graph = velocity

\[ \text{gradient} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = \text{velocity} \]
FeatureMeaning
Straight line, positive slopeConstant velocity, moving away from start.
Horizontal lineObject at rest (zero velocity).
Straight line, negative slopeConstant velocity, moving back toward start.
Curve steepeningVelocity increasing — accelerating.
Curve flatteningVelocity decreasing — decelerating.

Practice question

A hiker's displacement rises steadily from 0 m to 900 m over 30 minutes, then stays flat for 10 minutes. Describe the motion in each section, and calculate the hiker's velocity during the first section.
Practice
Model answer First 30 min: constant positive velocity, moving away from the start. Next 10 min: at rest (zero gradient). Velocity = 900/30 = 30 m per minute.
04

Velocity–Time Graphs

The two golden rules

  • Gradient = acceleration. Steeper = greater acceleration; flat = constant velocity; negative slope = deceleration.
  • Area under the graph = displacement. Split the shape into triangles, rectangles and trapeziums.
\[ a=\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} \qquad\qquad s = \text{area under the graph} \]

Worked example

A van accelerates from rest to 20 m/s in 5 s, holds 20 m/s for 10 s, then decelerates to rest over 5 s.

a (0–5s) = 20/5 = 4 m/s²  ·  a (15–20s) = −20/5 = −4 m/s²

Displacement = 50 + 200 + 50 = 300 m (two triangles + a rectangle)

Practice question

A scooter accelerates from rest to 12 m/s in 6 s, holds that speed for 8 s, then brakes to a stop in 4 s. Calculate the total distance travelled.
Practice
Model answer Triangle: ½×6×12=36 m. Rectangle: 8×12=96 m. Triangle: ½×4×12=24 m. Total = 156 m.
05

Instantaneous vs. Average Velocity

Average velocity is the overall displacement divided by the overall time taken for a journey — it hides any speeding up or slowing down that happened along the way. Instantaneous velocity is the velocity at one exact moment in time.

How to determine each, graphically

  • Average velocity between two points on a d–t graph = the gradient of the straight chord connecting those two points.
  • Instantaneous velocity at one point on a d–t graph = the gradient of the tangent touching the curve at that exact point.
  • On a v–t graph, instantaneous velocity is simply the y-value read off at that time; average velocity is the total area (displacement) divided by the total time.

For uniformly accelerated motion only, average velocity also equals the mean of the initial and final velocities:

\[ \bar{v} = \frac{u+v}{2} \]

Worked example

A car accelerates uniformly from 10 m/s to 30 m/s over 8 s. Average velocity = (10+30)/2 = 20 m/s, so distance travelled = 20 × 8 = 160 m.

Practice questions

1. Distinguish between instantaneous and average velocity, and explain how each is found from a displacement–time graph.
Practice
Model answer Instantaneous velocity is the velocity at one specific instant, found from the gradient of the tangent to the curve at that point. Average velocity is the overall rate over an interval, found from the gradient of the chord joining the start and end points of that interval (or total displacement ÷ total time).
2. A runner's position is 8 m at t=2s and 32 m at t=6s. Calculate their average velocity over this interval. If the runner was momentarily moving at exactly this speed at some point during the interval, would that be their instantaneous or average velocity at that moment?
Practice
Model answer Average velocity = (32−8)/(6−2) = 24/4 = 6 m/s. If the runner happens to be moving at exactly 6 m/s at some instant, that reading would be their instantaneous velocity at that moment — it's a coincidence that it matches the average, not the same concept.
06

Motion with Uniform & Non-Uniform Acceleration; the Equations of Motion

Type of accelerationWhat it looks like
UniformAcceleration is constant — velocity changes by equal amounts in equal time intervals. Straight line on a v–t graph.
Non-uniformAcceleration itself changes over time. Curved line on a v–t graph.

The equations of motion (SUVAT) — apply only to uniform acceleration

\[ v = u + at \qquad s = ut+\tfrac{1}{2}at^2 \qquad v^2=u^2+2as \qquad s=\left(\frac{u+v}{2}\right)t \]

Worked example — application

A car travelling at 25 m/s brakes with a uniform deceleration of 5 m/s². Find its braking distance.

\[ v^2=u^2+2as \Rightarrow 0 = 625 - 2(5)s \Rightarrow s = 62.5\text{ m} \]

Practice questions

1. A cyclist moving at 4 m/s accelerates uniformly at 1.5 m/s² for 6 s. Calculate the distance covered.
Practice
Model answer \(s=ut+\tfrac{1}{2}at^2=(4)(6)+\tfrac{1}{2}(1.5)(36)=24+27=51\text{ m}\).
2. Explain why the SUVAT equations cannot be used directly for a car easing to a stop in heavy stop-start traffic.
Practice
Model answer The SUVAT equations only apply when acceleration is uniform (constant). In stop-start traffic, the car's acceleration changes repeatedly and unpredictably (non-uniform), so a single value of "a" cannot describe the whole journey.

Forces, Free-Body Diagrams & Resultant Force

A free-body diagram isolates a single object and shows every force acting on it as an arrow, drawn from the object's centre, pointing in the force's direction, with its length roughly indicating its size.

ForceDescription
Gravitational force (weight)Pulls the object toward the Earth's centre; acts vertically downward, W = mg.
Normal force (FN)Support force a surface exerts perpendicular to itself.
Surface friction (Ff)Opposes relative sliding between two surfaces in contact.
TensionPulling force transmitted through a string, rope or cable.
Viscous dragResistive force opposing motion through a fluid — acts opposite to the direction of motion.
Buoyancy (upthrust)Resistive/supporting force a fluid exerts upward on a submerged or floating object.

Viscous drag & buoyancy — direction only

At this stage you only need to know the direction of viscous drag and buoyancy, and that both are examples of resistive forces (they oppose motion or oppose sinking) — not how to calculate their size.

Calculating resultant force — including planar (angled) forces

For forces along the same line, simply add (same direction) or subtract (opposite directions). For planar forces — two forces at an angle to each other, e.g. perpendicular — combine them the same way you combine vectors: with Pythagoras' theorem and trigonometry.

\[ R=\sqrt{F_1^2+F_2^2} \qquad \theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{F_2}{F_1}\right) \]

Worked example: A box experiences a 30 N horizontal force and a 40 N vertical force at the same time. \(R=\sqrt{30^2+40^2}=50\text{ N}\), at \(\theta=\tan^{-1}(40/30)\approx53.1°\) from the horizontal.

Practice questions

1. A book rests on a table. Draw (describe in words) the free-body diagram, naming each force and its direction.
Practice
Model answer Two forces: weight (W = mg) acting vertically downward from the book's centre, and the normal force (FN) acting vertically upward from the table's surface. Since the book is in equilibrium, these two arrows are equal in length and opposite in direction.
2. A swimmer experiences a forward thrust of 80 N and a sideways current force of 60 N, acting at right angles to each other. Calculate the magnitude and direction of the resultant force.
Practice
Model answer \(R=\sqrt{80^2+60^2}=\sqrt{10000}=100\text{ N}\). \(\theta=\tan^{-1}(60/80)\approx36.9°\) from the direction of thrust.

Newton's First Law & the Three Types of Inertia

Newton's First Law

An object remains at rest, or continues to move at constant velocity, unless acted upon by a resultant (net) external force. Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist a change in its state of motion — and mass is the measure of how much inertia an object has.

Type of inertiaDescriptionExample
Inertia of restA stationary object tends to stay stationary.A coin balanced on a card over a glass stays put when the card is flicked away sharply — then drops straight into the glass.
Inertia of motionA moving object tends to keep moving.A standing bus passenger lurches forward when the bus suddenly brakes.
Inertia of directionA moving object tends to keep moving in the same straight-line direction.Mud flung off a fast-spinning bicycle tyre flies off in a straight line, tangent to the wheel, rather than curving with it.

Practice questions

1. A tablecloth is yanked quickly from under dishes, leaving the dishes almost undisturbed on the table. Identify which type of inertia this demonstrates, and explain your answer.
Practice
Model answer Inertia of rest. The dishes are initially at rest, and the cloth is removed so quickly (with such little friction acting on the dishes) that there is not enough time for a significant resultant force to act on them — so by Newton's First Law they remain almost stationary.
2. A car travelling in a straight line suddenly turns sharply left. Explain, using Newton's First Law, why an unbelted passenger seems to be "thrown" toward the right-hand side of the car.
Practice
Model answer This is inertia of direction: the passenger's body tends to keep travelling in the original straight-line direction. As the car (and its seat) turns left beneath them, the passenger's body — with no direct force turning it — continues forward, which relative to the now-turning car looks like being flung toward the right.

Newton's Second Law & Linear Momentum

Two forms of the second law

\[ F = ma \qquad\qquad F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} \]

F = ma assumes the object's mass stays constant. F = Δp/Δt is the more general form — since \(p=mv\), this equals \(\Delta(mv)/\Delta t\), which still works even in situations where mass itself is changing (for example, a rocket burning and losing fuel mass as it accelerates). You won't be asked to calculate numerical examples where mass changes — just to understand why this form is more general.

Linear momentum

\[ p = mv \]

Momentum remains constant unless a resultant external force acts on the system (conservation of momentum).

Worked examples

F = ma: Force to accelerate a 1200 kg car at 2.5 m/s².

F = 1200 × 2.5 = 3000 N

F = Δp/Δt (mass constant): A 0.5 kg ball's speed changes from 2 m/s to 10 m/s in 0.4 s. Find the average force.

Δp = m·Δv = 0.5×(10−2) = 4 kg·m/s. F = 4/0.4 = 10 N

Practice questions

1. Explain why F = Δp/Δt is considered more general than F = ma.
Practice
Model answer F = ma only holds when mass stays constant. F = Δp/Δt = Δ(mv)/Δt accounts for momentum changing due to a change in velocity, a change in mass, or both — so it remains valid even when an object's mass is changing over time, a situation F = ma cannot describe.
2. A 2 kg trolley moving at 3 m/s collides and sticks to a stationary 1 kg trolley. Use conservation of momentum to find their common velocity after the collision.
Practice
Model answer Momentum before = (2×3)+(1×0) = 6 kg·m/s. After: (2+1)v = 6 → v = 2 m/s.

Impulse & Its Applications

Definition

A resultant external force applied to a system for a time Δt constitutes an impulse J:

\[ J = F\Delta t = \Delta p \]

The impulse applied to a system equals the change in its momentum. Rearranging shows that for a given change in momentum, a longer contact time means a smaller average force — this is the basis for most safety applications.

ApplicationHow it uses impulse
Crumple zones (cars)Deform and increase the time taken to stop in a crash, reducing the average force on occupants for the same change in momentum.
AirbagsIncrease the time over which a passenger's head/body decelerates, reducing the peak force experienced.
Safety padding (helmets, mats)Extends the collision time, lowering the average force transmitted to the body.
Follow-through in sportsExtending the contact time between bat/racket/foot and ball increases the impulse delivered for a given applied force — increasing the ball's final momentum (speed).

Two different goals, same formula

Safety devices (crumple zones, airbags, padding) increase Δt to reduce the force for a fixed, unavoidable Δp. Follow-through in sports increases Δt to increase the impulse (and so the ball's momentum) for a given force the athlete can apply.

Worked example

A 60 kg passenger is brought to rest from 20 m/s in a collision. Compare the average force if the collision lasts 0.1 s (rigid car) vs. 0.5 s (car with a crumple zone).

Δp = 60 × 20 = 1200 kg·m/s

Rigid: F = 1200/0.1 = 12,000 N

Crumple zone: F = 1200/0.5 = 2,400 N — a fivefold reduction

Practice questions

1. A 0.15 kg cricket ball travelling at 25 m/s is caught and brought to rest in 0.3 s. Calculate the impulse, and the average force on the fielder's hands.
Practice
Model answer Impulse = Δp = m×Δv = 0.15×25 = 3.75 kg·m/s (N·s). Force = J/Δt = 3.75/0.3 = 12.5 N.
2. Explain why a cricketer "gives" with their hands (draws them backward) while catching a fast-moving ball, using the concept of impulse.
Practice
Model answer The ball's change in momentum (impulse needed to stop it) is fixed. By drawing the hands back, the cricketer increases the time Δt over which the ball decelerates, which — since J=FΔt is constant — reduces the average force F on their hands, making the catch less painful and less likely to be dropped.
