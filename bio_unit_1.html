01

Negative Feedback Mechanism

Definitions

  • Homeostasis: The maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment within narrow, physiological limits despite fluctuations in external conditions.
  • Negative Feedback: A regulatory mechanism where a change in a physiological variable triggers a response that counteracts (reverses) the initial stimulus, bringing the system back towards its baseline set point.

Key components of every negative feedback loop:

  • Stimulus: A change in the internal or external environment (e.g., temperature rise).
  • Receptor (Sensor): Detects the change/stimulus and sends signals to the control center.
  • Control Center: Processes information, compares it to the set point, and sends signals to effectors (e.g., Hypothalamus or Pancreas).
  • Effector: An organ, gland, or muscle that executes a physiological response.
  • Response: The action taken by effectors that counteracts the original stimulus to restore balance.

02

Thermoregulation

Maintaining a constant core body temperature (approx. \(37^\circ\text{C}\)) is vital because enzymes require an optimal temperature to function efficiently. Extreme heat causes enzymes to denature (lose structural shape), while low temperatures drastically slow down metabolic reaction rates.

Effector / MechanismResponse to High Temperature (Overheating)Response to Low Temperature (Cooling Down)
Arterioles in Skin Vasodilation: Smooth muscles in arteriole walls relax, widening vessels near skin surface. Increases heat loss by radiation. Vasoconstriction: Smooth muscles contract, narrowing blood vessels near skin surface. Diverts warm blood to core organs.
Sweat Glands Sweat production increases. As liquid sweat evaporates from skin, it absorbs thermal energy (evaporative cooling). Sweat production drops to near zero to conserve thermal energy.
Skeletal Muscles Relaxed (no shivering). Shivering: Rapid, involuntary muscle contractions increase cellular respiration rates, releasing heat energy.
Hair Erector Muscles Relaxed, laying hair flat against skin so no insulating air boundary layer is trapped. Piloerection (Goosebumps): Muscles contract, pulling hairs upright to trap a layer of warm insulating air.
03

Glucose Regulation & Diabetes

The normal physiological range of blood glucose is roughly 4.0 – 7.0 mmol/L (or \(70 - 110\text{ mg/dL}\)). Blood glucose levels are controlled by two antagonistic hormones produced by the islets of Langerhans in the pancreas.

Hormonal Responses

  • High Blood Glucose (Hyperglycemia): Pancreas releases Insulin from Beta cells. Stimulates liver and muscle cells to take up glucose and convert it into Glycogen for storage.
  • Low Blood Glucose (Hypoglycemia): Pancreas releases Glucagon from Alpha cells. Stimulates liver cells to breakdown stored Glycogen into glucose and release it into the bloodstream.

04

Cardiovascular System in Homeostasis

  • Nutrient & Gas Transport: Hemoglobin in red blood cells delivers oxygen from lungs to respiring tissues, while blood plasma transports dissolved nutrients (glucose, amino acids) and removes metabolic waste (\(\text{CO}_2\), urea).
  • Temperature Regulation: Blood acts as a heat transport fluid, redistributing thermal energy from high-metabolism core organs (liver, heart) to skin capillaries for dissipation via vasodilation.
  • pH Balance: Maintains blood plasma pH within the tight range of \(7.35 - 7.45\) using bicarbonate buffer systems (\(\text{HCO}_3^-\)) to neutralize metabolic acids like carbonic acid.
05

Osmoregulation & Water Balance

Water serves as a universal solvent for metabolic biochemical reactions, maintains cellular volume and turgidity, and facilitates transport within plasma.

Water Movement Principles

  • Diffusion: Net movement of solute particles from high concentration to low concentration.
  • Osmosis: Net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential (lower solute concentration) to lower water potential (higher solute concentration) across a partially permeable membrane.

Role of Kidneys & ADH (Antidiuretic Hormone)

When body fluid concentration rises (dehydration), osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect the change and signal the pituitary gland to secrete ADH into the bloodstream.

  • High ADH Levels: Collecting ducts in kidneys become more permeable to water. More water is reabsorbed back into blood capillaries \(\rightarrow\) small volume of concentrated, dark urine produced.
  • Low ADH Levels: Collecting ducts become less permeable to water. Less water is reabsorbed \(\rightarrow\) large volume of dilute, clear urine produced.

Practice questions

1. Explain how vasodilation and sweating work together to decrease body temperature during intense physical exercise.
Cr: A, iiL 5–6
Model answer During exercise, cellular respiration increases, generating excess heat. The hypothalamus triggers vasodilation, causing smooth muscles in skin arterioles to relax. This increases blood flow to surface capillaries so heat dissipates by radiation. Simultaneously, sweat glands produce sweat; as water evaporates off the skin, it absorbs thermal energy from the blood, causing evaporative cooling.
2. Compare and contrast the causes and treatment strategies for Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes.
Cr: A, iiL 5–6
Model answer Comparison: Both conditions cause abnormally high blood glucose levels (hyperglycemia) due to impaired insulin action.
Differences: Type 1 is an autoimmune disease where the pancreas stops producing insulin, requiring lifelong daily insulin injections. Type 2 is characterized by insulin resistance in target cell receptors, often linked to lifestyle factors, and managed primarily through diet, exercise, or oral medication.
