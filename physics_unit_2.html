Kinematics & Motion

Kinematics describes the motion of objects using words, diagrams, numbers, graphs, and equations without considering the forces causing the motion.

Key Quantities: Scalars vs. Vectors

  • Distance (\(s\)): Total path length covered (Scalar, SI: \(\text{m}\)).
  • Displacement (\(s\)): Straight-line distance from initial to final position with direction (Vector, SI: \(\text{m}\)).
  • Speed (\(v\)): Rate of change of distance (Scalar, SI: \(\text{m s}^{-1}\)).
  • Velocity (\(v\)): Rate of change of displacement (Vector, SI: \(\text{m s}^{-1}\)).
  • Acceleration (\(a\)): Rate of change of velocity (Vector, SI: \(\text{m s}^{-2}\)).

Equations of Uniformly Accelerated Motion (SUVAT)

\[ v = u + at \quad | \quad s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2 \quad | \quad v^2 = u^2 + 2as \quad | \quad s = \frac{(u + v)}{2}t \]

u = initial velocity · v = final velocity · a = acceleration · t = time · s = displacement

Instantaneous vs. Average Velocity

Instantaneous velocity is the velocity of an object at a single specific moment in time (measured as the gradient of a tangent on a distance-time graph). Average velocity is the total displacement divided by total time: \(v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}\).

Motion graphs allow us to visually determine position, speed, velocity, acceleration, and distance travelled.

Understanding Displacement with Positive & Negative Velocities

When an object moves forward, velocity is positive (above the time axis). When it reverses direction, velocity becomes negative (below the time axis).

  • Total Distance: Sum of absolute area regions: \(\text{Area}_1 + |\text{Area}_2|\).
  • Total Displacement: Net area accounting for signs: \(\text{Area}_1 + \text{Area}_2\) (where region below axis is negative).

Practice questions

1. A sprinter runs along a straight track. Between \(t = 0\text{ s}\) and \(t = 4\text{ s}\), velocity increases uniformly from \(0\text{ m/s}\) to \(8\text{ m/s}\). From \(t = 4\text{ s}\) to \(t = 10\text{ s}\), velocity remains constant at \(8\text{ m/s}\).
(a) Calculate the acceleration during the first 4 seconds.
(b) Calculate the total distance covered in 10 seconds.
Cr: A, iiiL 3–4
Model answer (a) \(a = \frac{v - u}{t} = \frac{8 - 0}{4} = 2.0\text{ m/s}^2\)
(b) Distance = Area under \(v-t\) graph
\(\text{Area}_1 \text{ (triangle, 0–4 s)} = \frac{1}{2} \times 4 \times 8 = 16\text{ m}\)
\(\text{Area}_2 \text{ (rectangle, 4–10 s)} = 6 \times 8 = 48\text{ m}\)
\(\text{Total Distance} = 16 + 48 = 64\text{ m}\)
2. An object moves with a velocity of \(-3\text{ m/s}\) for 4 seconds, then turns and moves with a velocity of \(+2\text{ m/s}\) for 3 seconds.
(a) Determine the displacement during each segment.
(b) Calculate the total displacement and total distance travelled.
Cr: A, iiiL 5–6
Model answer (a) Segment 1: \(s_1 = (-3) \times 4 = -12\text{ m}\). Segment 2: \(s_2 = (+2) \times 3 = +6\text{ m}\).
(b) Total Displacement = \((-12) + (+6) = -6\text{ m}\) (6 metres in the negative direction).
Total Distance = \(|-12| + |+6| = 12 + 6 = 18\text{ m}\).
03

Newton's Laws & Inertia

Newton's First Law (Law of Inertia)

An object remains at rest or continues to move with constant velocity unless acted upon by a resultant (net) external force.

Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist a change in its state of motion. Mass is the direct measure of inertia — greater mass means greater inertia.

Newton's Second Law

The resultant force acting on an object is equal to the rate of change of momentum, or mass times acceleration:

\[ F_{\text{net}} = ma \quad \text{or} \quad F_{\text{net}} = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} \]

Newton's Third Law

If Object A exerts a force on Object B, Object B exerts an equal and opposite force on Object A. (Action-reaction pairs are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, and act on different bodies).

Practice questions

1. A bus is travelling at a constant velocity when the driver suddenly applies the brakes. The passengers lean forward.
(a) Identify the concept that explains why the passengers move forward.
(b) Explain the motion of the passengers using Newton's First Law.
(c) Explain how a seat belt helps protect a passenger in this situation.
Cr: A, i, iiL 5–6
Model answer (a) Inertia (or Newton's First Law of Motion).
(b) Before braking, passengers are moving forward at the bus's speed. When brakes are applied, a net external stopping force acts on the bus, but not directly on the passengers' upper bodies. Due to inertia, the passengers tend to maintain their constant forward velocity, moving forward relative to the decelerating bus.
(c) The seat belt provides the external unbalanced force needed to slow down the passenger along with the vehicle, preventing them from colliding with the dashboard or windshield.
2. A student wants to investigate how the type of surface affects the motion of a toy car rolling down a ramp.
(a) State a testable research question.
(b) Identify independent, dependent, and two controlled variables.
(c) Formulate a testable hypothesis using Newton's First Law / Friction.
Cr: B, i, iiiL 5–6
Model answer (a) Research Question: "How does changing the surface material (wood, cardboard, cloth) affect the distance travelled by a toy car after leaving a ramp?"
(b) Variables:
  • Independent: Type of surface material on the floor.
  • Dependent: Distance travelled by the toy car from the bottom of the ramp.
  • Controlled: Height/angle of ramp, mass of toy car, release point without pushing.
(c) Hypothesis: Smoother surfaces (wood) will allow the car to travel further because they produce lower friction. According to Newton's First Law, in the absence of a strong unbalanced resistive force (friction), an object will continue moving at near-constant velocity longer.

Momentum & Impulse

Momentum (\(p\))

\[ p = mv \]

p = momentum (kg m s⁻¹) · m = mass (kg) · v = velocity (m s⁻¹)

Impulse (\(J\)) and Momentum Change

Impulse is the product of force and time duration. It equals the change in momentum:

\[ J = F\Delta t = \Delta p = m(v - u) \]

Safety Applications of Impulse

In collisions (crumple zones, airbags, soft landing mats), the momentum change \(\Delta p\) is fixed. By extending the contact time \(\Delta t\), the impact force \(F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}\) is significantly reduced, minimizing injury.

Free Fall & Terminal Velocity

When an object falls through a fluid (like air), two main forces act on it: gravitational force (weight \(W = mg\)) pulling downwards, and air resistance (viscous drag \(F_d\)) opposing motion.

Phases of Motion to Terminal Velocity

  1. At release (\(t=0\)): Drag is zero (\(F_d = 0\)). Net force is weight downwards. Acceleration equals \(g \approx 9.81\text{ m/s}^2\).
  2. As speed increases: Air resistance \(F_d\) increases. Net force \(F_{\text{net}} = W - F_d\) decreases, so acceleration decreases.
  3. Terminal Velocity reached: Drag equals weight (\(F_d = W\)). Net force becomes zero (\(F_{\text{net}} = 0\)). Acceleration drops to zero, and the object continues falling at a constant maximum velocity.
06

Uniform Circular Motion

An object moving in a circle at a constant speed is continuously changing its direction. Because velocity is a vector, a changing direction means the object is continuously accelerating towards the centre.

Centripetal Acceleration & Force

\[ v = \frac{2\pi r}{T} \quad | \quad a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} \quad | \quad F_c = \frac{mv^2}{r} \]

The centripetal force is not a separate physical force; it is the net resultant force directed towards the centre of the circular path (e.g., tension in a string, friction on car tires, or gravity on planets).

Hooke's Law & Springs

Hooke's Law

The force exerted by a spring is directly proportional to its extension, provided the elastic limit is not exceeded:

\[ F_H = -kx \]

F_H = restoring force (N) · k = spring constant (N/m) · x = extension (m)

A higher spring constant \(k\) indicates a stiffer spring.

08

Moments & Equilibrium

Moment of a Force

\[ \text{Moment} = F \times d \]

F = force (N) · d = perpendicular distance from pivot (m)

Conditions for Static Equilibrium

  1. Resultant Force is Zero: \(\sum F = 0\) (Forces balance in all directions).
  2. Resultant Moment is Zero: \(\sum \text{Clockwise Moments} = \sum \text{Anticlockwise Moments}\).

Solid & Fluid Pressure

Solid Pressure Formula

\[ P = \frac{F}{A} \]

P = pressure (Pa or N/m²) · F = perpendicular force (N) · A = surface area (m²)

Fluid Pressure Formula

\[ P = h \rho g \]

h = depth of fluid column (m) · ρ = density of fluid (kg/m³) · g = acceleration due to gravity (N/kg)
